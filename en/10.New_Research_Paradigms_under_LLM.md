# New Research Paradigms under LLM

Large Language Models are profoundly transforming how computer science research is conducted. Earlier chapters have mentioned LLM's role in literature retrieval, paper writing, and code debugging, but those still remain at the level of "using tools to boost efficiency." As the entire research workflow gets accelerated, a fundamentally new paradigm is emerging — researchers no longer execute every experiment themselves, but instead write research directions, let AI Agents iterate autonomously, and provide directional guidance after reviewing the results.

This also means that research competition will become fiercer. The Human-on-the-Loop paradigm may make getting started in research "look" easier, but I hope that while embracing LLMs, you never let machines think for you. Reading papers and building technical knowledge — these traditional research training methods can at least help you develop your own research framework. If everything is delegated to LLMs, especially for research beginners, you may achieve decent experimental results, but the actual growth in your own capabilities remains limited. This is also why "AI-curated knowledge bases" have recently become popular — while people are deep into vibe coding, they are also growing anxious about their core competitiveness and knowledge systems. After all, tools are just tools; your future still depends on your own initiative.

LLM development moves fast, and its impact on research deepens by the day — LLMs have permeated every aspect of work and life. Precisely because of this, the content of this chapter may already be outdated in a few months. Therefore, this chapter will not cover too many specific tool operations. We encourage you to independently explore the latest LLMs and programming tools. The following focuses on the Human-on-the-Loop paradigm.

## Starting from autoresearch

In March 2025, Andrej Karpathy released [autoresearch](https://github.com/karpathy/autoresearch) (70k+ GitHub stars), a project of merely ~630 lines of code that sent shockwaves through the research community. Its core idea is remarkably simple:

> Agent modifies `train.py` → trains for 5 minutes → checks if validation metric improved → keeps or reverts → repeats

At a pace of roughly 12 experiments per hour, Karpathy let the Agent run autonomously overnight. In 2 days, it completed approximately 700 experiments, discovered 20 effective optimizations, and achieved an 11% training speed improvement. Shopify CEO Tobias Lütke immediately applied the same approach to the company's templating engine — through 37 automated experiments and 93 automated commits, rendering performance improved by 53%.

This workflow was later summarized by analyst Janakiram MSV as **"The Karpathy Loop"**, with three core elements:

1. A code file the Agent can access and modify
2. A single, objectively measurable optimization metric
3. A fixed time budget per experiment

The real insight of autoresearch is not automation itself, but how it redefines the researcher's role. The project has only three core components:

- `program.md`: Natural language experiment instructions written by the researcher — defining goals, constraints, and evaluation criteria
- `train.py`: The only file the Agent can modify — model definition and training logic
- `prepare.py`: Data preparation (immutable infrastructure)

Researchers no longer write training code manually but instead **"program" the experimental process in natural language**. This creates a fascinating chain: humans write natural language descriptions → LLM translates to Python modifications → Python trains neural networks. In Karpathy's words: "All LLM frontier labs will do this. It's the final boss battle."

The academic community has corresponding evaluation work: [MLAgentBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03302) (Huang et al., 2023) provides a benchmark for evaluating Agents on ML experimentation; [ResearchAgent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07738) (Baek et al., 2024) explores knowledge-graph-based iterative idea generation; a [large-scale study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.04109) involving 100+ NLP researchers (Si et al., 2024) found that LLM-generated ideas scored higher on novelty than human experts, but lower on feasibility.

## Human-on-the-Loop

What autoresearch embodies is essentially the **Human-on-the-Loop** research paradigm — unlike traditional Human-in-the-Loop (where every step requires human approval), in Human-on-the-Loop mode the Agent operates autonomously within a defined scope while the human monitors and redirects at key checkpoints.

However, there are also important issues to watch out for:

- **Verify results**: Agent-generated code may contain subtle bugs. Every critical result must be verified by a human.
- **Understand your experiments**: Don't let the Agent become a black box. Even if the Agent ran the experiment, you must fully understand the design and code logic — reviewers care about your depth of understanding.
- **Document reproducibility info**: Record the prompts used, code versions, and runtime environment to ensure reproducibility.
- **Academic integrity**: Follow target venue policies on LLM usage. Take full responsibility for paper content and disclose LLM usage.

## LLM-Assisted Research Toolkit

In addition to writing tools and programming tools covered in earlier chapters, these tools are also worth exploring:

**Literature discovery & synthesis**: [Elicit](https://elicit.com/), [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/), [Consensus](https://consensus.app/), [Research Rabbit](https://www.researchrabbit.ai/)

**Idea generation & evaluation**: LLMs can serve as brainstorming partners — the effective approach is structured exploration on a foundation of domain knowledge (describe existing method limitations, ask LLM for improvement directions); don't expect to simply ask an LLM to "give me a top-conference idea." What comes from LLMs alone remains superficial; true understanding requires hands-on work. Reading papers and running demo experiments for validation — these traditional approaches are still essential. After all, an LLM's research literacy is not the same as your research literacy.

**Research programming & debugging**: GitHub Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code, and similar tools can accelerate code writing, assist with debugging, and help understand code. Currently, the top tier is Claude Code paired with the Claude Opus model, followed by high-volume options like Codex and GLM. Claude Code itself is just a tool that can connect to any model. You can also try the open-source OpenCode tool, which makes it easier to connect to various providers.

## Recommendations for Graduate Students

1. **Tools are means, not ends.** A paper's core competitiveness comes from your understanding of the problem and innovation of methods, not from the tools you use.
2. **Build fundamentals first, then leverage tools.** In early research, build solid foundations through traditional methods. Only with independent judgment can you effectively use LLMs and identify their errors.
3. **Maintain critical thinking.** LLMs may fabricate references, generate incorrect code, or provide overly optimistic analyses. Always maintain the habit of verification.
4. **Stay updated on venue policies.** Policies on LLM usage at major conferences and journals are still evolving rapidly. Always check the latest requirements before submission.
